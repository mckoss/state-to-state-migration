About the ± numbers

The state-to-state flows come from the American Community Survey (ACS), which asks a sample of about 1 in 40 homes each year where everyone lived a year earlier. The Census Bureau scales the answers up to the whole population, so every number is an estimate, and it publishes a margin of error with each one. “2,963 ± 1,488” means the Bureau is 90% confident the true number is between 1,475 and 4,451.

Why the margins can be so large

  • Few people in the sample made that move. Only about 2% of people move to another state in a year, and those moves are spread over 2,652 pairs of states. A flow of a few thousand people rests on a few dozen survey answers, so it can't be pinned down closely. Big flows are much more precise: California → Texas in 2024 was 77,161 ± 9,059 (±12%), while Massachusetts → Washington was 2,963 ± 1,488 (±50%). In 2024, more than a quarter of the state-to-state flows have a margin as large as the estimate itself.
  • Zero doesn't mean nobody. When no one in the sample made a move, the estimate is 0, but the margin (for example, Wyoming → Vermont: 0 ± 170) says it could still be a few hundred people.
  • Net numbers are the least certain. A net is the difference between two flows that are often similar in size. The flows mostly cancel, but their uncertainties don't: they combine. Washington's exchange with Massachusetts in 2024 was 2,963 in and 2,252 out, a net of +711, but the margin on the net is ±1,755 — bigger than the net itself. The true net could be a loss of 1,044 or a gain of 2,466, so the map shows it in gray.

How to read them here

  • When the margin is larger than the estimate, the number is not statistically significant: the survey can't rule out zero. On the map those arrows are dashed and unlabeled, uncertain nets are gray, and in the tables the values are gray italic. Hover for the full range.
  • Totals and longer periods are more precise. A state's total moves combine dozens of flows, and the combined 5- and 10-survey datasets add up independent samples, so their margins are smaller relative to the estimates.
  • The population estimates in the bottom section show no margins. They aren't a survey: the Census Bureau builds them from tax returns, Medicare enrollment and other records, and doesn't publish margins of error for them. They're not exact either.

Sources and notes

  • Flows: U.S. Census Bureau, State-to-State Migration Flows, American Community Survey 1-year estimates, 2005–2024. No 2020 table was released, because 2020 ACS 1-year data did not meet quality standards. "Current residence" is the destination and "residence 1 year ago" the origin. Estimates carry a 90% margin of error (shown as ±); small flows are often not statistically different from zero. Margins for a state's total movers use the Census Bureau's published figures where the tables include them (in-movers from 2010, out-movers from 2024); otherwise, and for net figures, they are approximated as the square root of the sum of the squared margins. A net is shown in gray, without a number on the map, when its margin of error is larger than the net itself. The combined datasets add up the most recent 5 or 10 yearly tables, so they count moves rather than people (someone who moved twice counts twice); their margins of error combine the same way. Cells marked N were suppressed by the Census Bureau and are treated as zero on the map.
  • Population estimates: U.S. Census Bureau, Vintage 2025 state population totals and components of change (NST-EST2025-ALLDATA). Each year runs July 1 to June 30; 2020 covers April 1 to June 30, 2020 only. Rates are per 1,000 residents.
  • All data is served from copies stored with this site; nothing is fetched from census.gov at run time. The copies are checked against the Census Bureau monthly and updated when new data is published.
